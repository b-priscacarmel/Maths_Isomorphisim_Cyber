Detecting a botnet through iterative colour refinement

Every node starts coloured only by its degree. Each round, a node recomputes its colour from its own colour plus the sorted colours of its neighbours — this is the 1-Weisfeiler-Lehman algorithm, and it's the exact mechanism a Graph Isomorphism Network layer performs. After enough rounds, a device's colour is a certificate of its local structural role. A botnet hub is found by matching certificates against the known signature's hub certificate.

Why this matters: two structures can only be truly isomorphic if their WL colours match at every round — so an exact colour match is a strong (though not perfect) isomorphism certificate. Route the attack through a relay and the hub's neighbourhood colours change, breaking the certificate — showing precisely why GIN alone isn't unbeatable, and why detection systems combine it with other signals.

This mirrors Xu et al.'s theoretical result (the paper that introduced GIN): a GIN's expressive power is bounded above by the 1-WL test — it can never distinguish structures 1-WL can't, but with training it approximates this refinement over huge graphs where exact backtracking search is infeasible. Yin et al.'s IoT detector trades our exact string-certificate matching for a learned, tolerant version of the same colour-refinement idea, at a scale of millions of devices.
